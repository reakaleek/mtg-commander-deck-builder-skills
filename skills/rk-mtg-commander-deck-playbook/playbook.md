# Playbook structure

Fill each section from the submitted list and the interview. Do not prescribe
turn numbers or category quotas. Do not name cards here.

## Deck identity

What resource does this deck create, convert, and spend to win?

State the plan in plain language before listing lines.

## Mulligans

Which opening-hand roles does this list actually need?

Keep or mulligan from those roles, not from a generic hand recipe.

## Sequencing

Describe early, middle, and late play as setup, conversion, and close.

Do not assign those stages to numbered turns. Commander timing lives in the
next section; do not repeat it here.

## Commander timing

When does casting the commander advance the plan?

When does it expose the commander to removal or tax without enough payoff?

## Commander tax decision

When the commander dies, when do you recast through tax, and when do you
move on with cards in the 99?

## Spare mana

What should leftover mana do on a typical turn: hold up interaction, deploy
another piece, or convert?

## Synergy packages

Group pieces that work together.

Explain how their Oracle texts interact. Separate a locked line from a nice
overlap.

## Combo and win lines

Write each close as ordered steps.

Include prerequisites, what the table can interrupt, and what you still have
after a stop.

Tutors only appear in a line if the deck contains the cards they would find.

## Closing math

For each win line, what a lethal turn needs against three opponents at 40
life. Say the damage, drain, or mill required, and which pieces supply it.

## Interaction discipline

What to hold answers for, and when to pass with mana up.

Ground each point in this list's answers and the pod they described:

- **Behind or ahead.** Answers matter most when the pilot is behind, which is
  most of the game at a table of four. Say when this deck, once ahead, should
  spend the turn pushing the win instead of answering.
- **Scarce and plentiful.** Which answer types are scarce in this list and
  must be saved for a real problem, and which are plentiful enough to spend
  freely. The more answers the list runs overall, the more freely the pilot
  can fire them.
- **When to fire.** Spend an answer once there is no doubt the threat is
  aimed at this pilot (an attack declared at them, a trigger about to
  resolve). Name the threats whose cast or attack triggers force an earlier
  answer. Say when taking damage is better than losing a card.
- **Where to hit.** In the opposing strategies they described, name the
  engine pieces whose removal switches off several other cards. Hitting
  those beats answering whatever is biggest.
- **Two-for-ones.** Which instant-speed answers in this list can wait for a
  response that strands a second card: an aura, equipment, pump or fight
  spell aimed at the target.
- **Early or late.** Which answers to fire early to slow a snowballing engine
  or fast mana, and which to hold for the closing turn to clear blockers or
  a lock. Temporary answers (bounce, tuck, exile that ends) are worth the
  most early, when the lost tempo costs the opponent the most.
- **Mana exchange.** Flag answers that cost the pilot's whole turn, and say
  when that trade isn't worth it against a cheap threat.
- **Creatures as answers.** Name the creatures, the commander included, that
  double as answers by blocking or trading, and when the trade is worth it.
- **Answers that can backfire.** For fight effects and answers that target
  the pilot's own creature, say what must be true before casting, since the
  opponent can respond and turn it into a two-for-one against the pilot.
- **Mana up with a fallback.** If the list passes with mana up for answers,
  name the instant-speed play that uses that mana when nothing needs
  answering.

Skip generic "always hold a counter" advice that would apply to any deck.

## Pivot and recovery

What do you do after removal, a wipe, graveyard hate, commander tax, or a
stopped win attempt?

Name the remaining engines in this list. Do not invent a backup that is not
in the file.

## Threat assessment and politics

Use the playgroup they described.

Who do you need to answer, and when does this deck want to look smaller than
it is?

Say how it hides its power: how much of its winning power sits in hand rather
than on the battlefield, which finishers to hold until the turn they win, and
which small pieces look harmless to the table. A deck that sits as the obvious
threat for several turns gets attacked first.

Use how the table usually treats this pilot. If the pod targets them early,
say which setup pieces to hold back and which answers to deploy first. If the
pilot usually goes unnoticed, say when the table will turn on them and what
to hold for that moment.

When it's unclear which threat matters most, ask the table what their pieces
do before spending an answer. A wrong answer still teaches what the opposing
decks hold.

## What beats this deck

Which plans, hate pieces, or table shapes this list loses to, and what the
pilot can still do from the cards that remain.

## Rules reminders

Call out triggers, stack order, replacement effects, and timing only where
this list's Oracle text makes them easy to miss.

## Quick reference

A compact at-table card: keep, sequence, protect, close, recover.

Keep it short enough to glance at between turns.
